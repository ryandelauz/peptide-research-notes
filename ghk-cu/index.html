GHK-Cu: A Research Reference on the Copper Tripeptide, From Plasma Discovery to Lab Reagent

Research use only: GHK-Cu sold as a research compound is not an approved drug or supplement and is not for human or veterinary use.

Research context: this reference summarises published chemistry, cell-culture and animal literature, and the limited human research, for laboratory researchers. It contains no guidance on human use, dosing or preparation for administration.

Disclosure: this overview was prepared on behalf of Peptide Titans, a research-peptide supplier, and links once to its product page.

Short answer: GHK-Cu is the copper(II) complex of the tripeptide glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine (GHK). The tripeptide was first characterised as a human plasma factor that changed how cultured cells grew. It binds copper tightly, and a 1988 fibroblast-culture study reported that the copper complex stimulated collagen synthesis. Since then, a large literature has built up, ranging from cell biology to computational gene-expression analyses. Most of it is in vitro or animal work. Human research is mainly topical and cosmetic, and many broad claims made about GHK-Cu rest on review articles rather than controlled experiments.

Origins: a plasma factor that changed how cells grew

The GHK story starts with Loren Pickart and colleagues in the 1970s.

  • 1979: in FEBS Letters, Pickart and Thaler described a growth-modulating human plasma tripeptide and examined how its structure related to DNA synthesis in hepatoma cells.
  • 1980: in the Journal of Cellular Physiology, the same authors reported that the tripeptide (then abbreviated GHL) is complexed with copper and iron in plasma. At physiological pH in vitro, it associated with several transition metals, but not with calcium, potassium or sodium. In cultures of a hepatoma cell line, the tripeptide–metal complexes changed cell adhesion and growth, with copper and iron the most active.
  • 1981: in a paper in In Vitro, Pickart summarised the tripeptide's use in culture systems and proposed that it acts as a transporter of transition metals, particularly copper, to the cell surface.

A 2018 review by Pickart and Margolina dates the discovery to 1973.

Chemistry: what "GHK-Cu" actually refers to

  • Free peptide: GHK alone is C14H24N6O4, molecular weight 340.38 (PubChem CID 73587).
  • Copper complex: in GHK-Cu, the tripeptide coordinates a Cu(II) ion. In a computational study combining ligand-field molecular mechanics, DFT and semi-empirical methods, Alshammari and Platts (2020) found that four equatorial bonds (a 3N1O coordination) stayed stable throughout simulation. A fifth, apical bond from the C-terminal carboxylate was more fluxional.
  • Different ways of writing the same complex: supplier and database records do not always agree on the formula, because the complex can be written as neutral or charged and with different protonation. One supplier page lists C14H22CuN6O4 (about 401.9 Da) under CAS 89030-95-5. PubChem's record for the same CAS number gives a cationic formula, C14H23CuN6O4+, at 402.92.

That last point matters for identity testing. The expected mass depends on which species the method measures. A mass-spectrometry result may show the free peptide, the copper-bound ion, or both, depending on conditions. When you read a certificate, check which species the "expected mass" refers to.

What cell and animal studies have reported

Collagen and matrix synthesis in culture

The most-cited single experiment is Maquart et al. (1988, FEBS Letters). GHK-Cu stimulated collagen synthesis in fibroblast cultures at very low concentrations, and the effect was independent of any change in cell number. The authors noted that the GHK triplet occurs in the α2(I) chain of type I collagen. From that, they hypothesised that proteases could release the tripeptide at a wound site.

Pickart, Vasquez-Soltero and Margolina (2015) reviewed the follow-on work. They summarised reports that GHK modulates both synthesis and breakdown of collagen and glycosaminoglycans and affects metalloproteinases and their inhibitors. They also cited animal studies in rats, mice and pigs.

Gene-expression analyses

A large share of recent GHK discussion comes from analyses using the Broad Institute's Connectivity Map. As Pickart and Margolina (2018) describe it, this is a public library of transcriptional responses to known compounds. Researchers used it to look for compounds whose expression signature opposed disease-associated profiles, and GHK appeared in analyses linked to emphysematous lung tissue.

These are hypothesis-generating, computational associations. Statements such as "GHK regulates thousands of genes" describe expression-profile data. They do not show functional effects in a living system.

A note on authorship

The author affiliations on the broad 2015 and 2018 reviews list a skin-care research and development company. That does not by itself undermine the data. It is still a reason to rely on the primary experiments rather than the reviews' general conclusions.

Human research: mostly topical, mostly cosmetic

GHK-Cu has a long history as a cosmetic ingredient. The 2015 review summarises cosmetic studies, and our searches suggest the human research base is still small and mostly topical.

On 12 November 2026, ClinicalTrials.gov listed a handful of registered studies mentioning GHK-Cu. They include a recruiting, vehicle-controlled study of a topical GHK-Cu gel and a planned study measuring circulating GHK and GHK-Cu levels in healthy adults. Registration describes study design, not outcomes.

Delivery through skin is itself an open research problem. Ogórek et al. (2025, Molecules) note that GHK-Cu is fairly hydrophilic and permeates poorly through the lipophilic stratum corneum. Liposomal encapsulation may help, but they found that the transport of liposome-encapsulated GHK-Cu has received little methodological attention.

Open questions

  1. Metal speciation in experiments. Is the effect being measured due to the copper complex, the free peptide, or copper delivery? Studies do not always separate these.
  2. Reproducibility of the gene-expression story. Database-derived signatures need confirmation in controlled experiments with defined material.
  3. Concentration relevance. Culture effects at very low concentrations raise questions about how stable the complex is in different media and buffers.
  4. Topical delivery. How much intact complex crosses skin under realistic conditions is not well characterised.

Handling, storage and identity considerations for researchers

  • Appearance is not identity. Copper complexes are coloured, and supplier pages typically describe a blue to blue-violet powder. Colour suggests copper is present. It does not confirm the peptide sequence, the copper-to-peptide ratio or the purity.
  • Ask what was measured. A useful certificate states the HPLC purity method, an MS identity result with the species it refers to, and ideally copper content. If your work depends on the complex staying intact, ask whether the analytical method preserves it or measures peptide and metal separately.
  • Moisture and solutions. Bachem's handling guidance notes that peptides tend to be hygroscopic and recommends warming a sealed container to room temperature in a desiccator before opening. It also recommends aliquoting solutions and freezing them below −15 °C rather than storing them long-term.
  • Supplier documentation, one example. The product page at peptidetitans.com lists GHK-Cu as the copper(II) complex of Gly-His-Lys, in 50 mg and 100 mg lyophilised vials. Each lot has a certificate of analysis reporting HPLC purity and mass-spectrometry identity, and recommended long-term storage is −20 °C to −80 °C, protected from light and moisture. With any supplier, tie the vial's lot number to its certificate before use.
  • Free GHK vs GHK-Cu. They are different materials with different formulas. Make sure the methods section, the label and the certificate all name the same one.

Bottom line

GHK-Cu is a small, well-defined copper complex with a five-decade research history. That history includes solid early cell-culture chemistry, a broad and partly computational review literature, and a thin, mainly topical human evidence base. For laboratory work, its chemistry is the place to be careful: define whether you are using the complex or the free peptide, confirm identity by mass with the species stated, and keep cosmetic or clinical claims separate from what your experiment can show.
